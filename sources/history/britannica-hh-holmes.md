- Who was H.H. Holmes?







H.H. Holmes, born Herman Webster Mudgett (1861-1896), is widely considered America’s first known serial killer. Operating under the alias H.H. Holmes, he was a swindler and confidence trickster. In Chicago, Holmes constructed a three-story hotel equipped with secret passages, trapdoors, soundproof rooms, and a kiln to cremate bodies; this became known as his "Murder Castle". During the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, he lured victims, often women he engaged, and murdered them after gaining control of their assets. Holmes also took out life insurance policies on his employees, naming himself as the beneficiary. After confessing to 27 murders, Holmes was sentenced to death and hanged in 1896.

**H.H. Holmes** (born May 16, 1861?, Gilmanton, [New Hampshire](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Hampshire-state), U.S.—died May 7, 1896, [Philadelphia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Philadelphia), Pennsylvania) was an American swindler and confidence trickster who is widely considered the country’s first known serial killer.

Mudgett was born into a wealthy family and showed signs of high intelligence from an early age. Always interested in medicine, he allegedly trapped animals and performed surgery on them; some accounts of his life even suggest that he killed a childhood playmate. Mudgett attended medical school at the [University of Michigan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/University-of-Michigan), where he was a [mediocre](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/mediocre) student. In 1884 he was nearly prevented from graduating when a widowed hairdresser accused him of making a false promise of marriage to her.

In 1886 Mudgett moved to [Chicago](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chicago) and took a job as a pharmacist under the name “Dr. H.H. Holmes.” Soon afterward he apparently began killing people in order to steal their property. The house he built for himself, which would become known as “Murder Castle,” was equipped with secret passages, trapdoors, soundproof rooms, doors that could be locked from the outside, gas jets to asphyxiate victims, and a kiln to cremate the bodies. At the reputed peak of his career, during the [World’s Columbian Exposition](https://www.britannica.com/event/Worlds-Columbian-Exposition) in Chicago in 1893, he allegedly seduced and murdered a number of women, typically by becoming engaged to them and then killing them after securing control of their life savings. Mudgett also required his employees to carry [life insurance](https://www.britannica.com/money/life-insurance) policies naming him as [beneficiary](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/beneficiary) so that he could collect money after he killed them. He sold the bodies of many of his victims to local medical schools.

In 1893 Mudgett was arrested for insurance fraud after a fire at his home, but he was soon released. He then concocted a scheme with an associate, Ben Pitezel, to defraud an insurance company by faking Pitezel’s death. After Pitezel purchased a $10,000 life insurance policy, he and Mudgett traveled to Colorado, Missouri, [New York](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-York-state), [Pennsylvania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pennsylvania-state), Tennessee, and [Texas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Texas-state), where they committed other acts of fraud (along the way, Mudgett also married). Returning to Missouri, Mudgett was arrested for fraud and briefly jailed in St. Louis. While in jail he met Marion Hedgepeth, a [career criminal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/recidivism) who agreed to help Mudgett in the [insurance](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/insurance) scheme with Pitezel. Meanwhile, Pitezel moved to Philadelphia and opened a fake patent office to swindle inventors. After his release from jail, Mudgett traveled to Philadelphia and killed Pitezel. He then convinced Pitezel’s widow, who had been aware of her husband’s involvement in the insurance scheme, that her husband was still alive, later giving her $500 of the money he collected. Worried that some of Pitezel’s five children might alert the authorities, Mudgett killed three of them. Insurance investigators were alerted to the fraud by Hedgepeth, and Mudgett was arrested in [Boston](https://www.britannica.com/place/Boston), [Massachusetts](https://www.britannica.com/place/Massachusetts), in 1894. He was tried in Philadelphia for the [murder](https://www.britannica.com/topic/murder-crime) of Pitezel and was sentenced to death by [hanging](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hanging).

Mudgett confessed to 27 murders (he later increased the total to more than 130), though some researchers have suggested that the [real number](https://www.britannica.com/science/real-number) [exceeded](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/exceeded) 200\. Mudgett sold his story to the Hearst Corporation for $10,000.

### What is the definition of a serial killer?

According to the [FBI](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Federal-Bureau-of-Investigation), serial killers (also called serial murderers) are criminals who have committed at least two homicides in separate events and at different times. Individuals who commit multiple murders in a single incident are considered mass murderers.

### What country has the most serial killers?

The United States has the most documented serial killers in the world. According to various sources, the country has had more than 3,500 serial killers since 1900. The next closest countries are Russia and the United Kingdom, which both have more than 190 serial killers.

### How many serial killers are never caught?

While the exact number is unknown, numerous serial killers are never caught. Notable examples of unidentified serial killers include [Jack the Ripper](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jack-the-Ripper), who murdered at least five women in London in 1888; the [Zodiac killer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Zodiac-killer), who is believed to have murdered at least five people in northern California between 1968 and 1969; and the [Monster of Florence](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Monster-of-Florence), a killer or killers who murdered at least 16 people in the hills outside Florence, Italy, between 1968 and 1985. The latter case inspired Thomas Harris’s novel _Hannibal_ (1999).

Learn about [five notorious cold cases in the United States](https://www.britannica.com/list/americas-5-most-notorious-cold-cases-including-one-you-may-have-thought-was-already-solved).

### Who are some notable American serial killers?

Notable American serial killers include [David Berkowitz](https://www.britannica.com/biography/David-Berkowitz) (“Son of Sam”), who killed 6 people in New York City in 1976–77; [Ted Bundy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ted-Bundy), a killer and rapist who confessed to 28 murders but may have actually killed hundreds in the late 20th century; [Jeffrey Dahmer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jeffrey-Dahmer), who murdered 17 people and engaged in [cannibalism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cannibalism-human-behavior) and necrophilia; [John Wayne Gacy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Wayne-Gacy), whose killing of 33 men in the 1970s shocked his community, where he performed as a clown at charity events; and [Ed Gein](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ed-Gein), who inspired the horror films [_Psycho_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Psycho-film-1960) (1960), _Texas Chainsaw Massacre_ (1974), and [_The Silence of the Lambs_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Silence-of-the-Lambs) (1991).

[Serial killers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/serial-murder), or serial murderers, are criminals who have committed at least two [homicides](https://www.britannica.com/topic/homicide) over a period of time.
