## Batch summary
- Total facts reviewed: 212
- Total PASS / REVISE / BORDERLINE / REJECT: 140 / 6 / 62 / 4
- % of batch not meeting full quality bar (REVISE+BORDERLINE+REJECT): 72/212 = 34%
- Strongest categories in this batch: **famous-disasters** (24/27 PASS, 89%) and **explorers** (25/30 PASS, 83%). Both categories lean on narrative, human stakes, and vivid physical detail (tsunamis, shipwrecks, mutinies, betrayals) that translate instantly across ages.
- Weakest categories in this batch: **internet-culture** (4/11 PASS, 36%) and **superstitions** (10/26 PASS, 38%). Internet-culture facts frequently read as tech-history trivia rather than "wait, really?" moments. Superstitions facts frequently explain *why* a belief exists (etymology/origin) rather than delivering a surprising payoff, and the category has heavy topic duplication.
- Most common failure patterns observed:
  1. **Informative-not-astonishing trivia** — facts that teach a mildly interesting historical/etymological nugget but never escalate to genuine surprise (e.g., word origins, "here's where a custom comes from").
  2. **Textbook/explainer framing** — hooks structured as "Why X Happens" or "X Tells You Y," which the brief explicitly flags as a weak pattern (zero-nothing-and-number, calculus-tiny-pieces-whole).
  3. **Within-topic redundancy** — many topics were sliced into 3 facts where only 1 is genuinely strong and the other 2 are supporting footnotes (Post-it Notes, Safety Pin, Ballpoint Pen, Barcode, Evil Eye, Black Cats, Rabbit's Foot, Broken Mirrors, Knocking on Wood, Mosasaurs).
  4. **Adult/technical/economic framing** — facts that require understanding patents, licensing, economics, or geopolitics before the surprise lands (Friday 13th's economic cost, GPS Selective Availability, Nash equilibrium/game theory as economics, QR code patent strategy).
  5. **Abstract math concepts without a strong visual anchor** — proofs and symbol-history facts (Euclid's infinite-primes proof, the integral sign's origin, fractional dimension) that are intellectually neat but hard for a lay reader, let alone a child, to picture or feel.
- Topics that feel oversaturated (too many similar facts in this batch): Post-it Notes (3), Safety Pin (3), Ballpoint Pen (3), Barcode (3), Evil Eye (3), Black Cats (3), Rabbit's Foot (3), Broken Mirrors (3), Mosasaurs (3), Knocking on Wood (2), Zero (3, two of which are weak explainers), Fibonacci/Golden Ratio cross-topic (fact #48 duplicates the "divide neighboring Fibonacci numbers" reveal already covered by #22 and #24).
- Categories relying too heavily on adult/technical/economic context: **mathematics** (game theory framed via Nash/economics, the Euclid proof, calculus notation history) and pockets of **superstitions** (Friday the 13th's "economic cost" framing) and **inventions** (GPS Selective Availability, QR code patent-licensing strategy) lean on concepts — proofs, patents, licensing, macroeconomics — that require adult context to feel meaningful rather than merely informative.
